Design Brief

Context that frames the whole Criterion B response for this lesson.

Problem context (real world & UAE link): A robot guarding a school entrance or mosque car park needs to look around, zoom on a face or number plate and keep the picture steady.

Design task: Design a physical, 3D-printable / laser-cut module that integrates the UGOT Camera module + pan/tilt servos and bolts to the UGOT chassis. It must be buildable by a Grade 10 student in the Product Design lab (MDF, wood, 3D printing).

User: a 14–16 year-old MYP student assembling, testing and demonstrating the robot in class and at the AIA STEM showcase.

B1 · Design Specification

Each success criterion is described and justified using ACCESSFM. Rows marked      are measurable. Size, mass and cost limits are calculated from the chosen 3D model.

Specification#Success criterionJustification
Aesthetics1.1 Colour scheme of pearl white and dark glass with a blue lens glow.A high-visibility, consistent palette makes the tool instantly recognisable and looks professional at the AIA STEM showcase.
1.2 Smooth, rounded external edges (fillet ≥ 2 mm) with no visible fasteners on the outer shell.Rounded edges read as safe to handle and avoid snagging.
Cost2.1 Material + fabrication cost per unit ≤ AED 70.Based on PETG filament at about AED 0.12/g, MDF offcuts and AED 14 of hardware; keeps a class set affordable.
2.2 Re-uses UGOT kit servos, sensors and fasteners; no proprietary parts.Avoids extra purchase orders and keeps the design compatible with kits the school already owns.
Customer3.1 Bolts to the UGOT chassis with M3 fasteners through the mount plate using only the lab toolkit (check hole spacing against your own kit).Every group's UGOT chassis must be able to accept the part.
3.2 Assembles in one lesson (≤ 70 min) by a pair of Grade 10 students.Matches the AIA single-period lesson structure for this unit.
Environment4.1 At least 80% of the structural material is recyclable PETG or reclaimed MDF offcuts.PETG is recyclable and MDF offcuts come from the lab scrap bin (6 R's: Reduce, Reuse).
4.2 Printed + cut mass ≤ 200 g.Lower mass means less filament, less print energy and less load on the UGOT motors.
Size5.1 Overall envelope ≤ 120 × 130 × 150 mm (L × W × H).Keeps the module compact enough to mount on the UGOT chassis without hitting its wheels or the floor.
5.2 Every printed part fits on a 200 × 200 mm print bed; every cut part fits a 300 × 300 mm laser bed.Matches the lab machinery available to students.
Safety6.1 No exposed wiring; servo gaps are covered so fingers cannot be caught.Reduces the chance of injury to the student building, testing and demonstrating it.
6.2 No exposed sharp corners: all edges filleted or sanded; sensor lens recessed about 2 mm to avoid impact cracks.Standard safe-handling requirement for a student-built robotic part.
Function7.1 Pan covers 180° and tilt covers 60° in under 3 seconds.Directly measures whether the design solves the problem in the scenario above.
7.2 Camera view is never blocked by the housing across its full pan and tilt range.Sensor range figures are design targets; the housing must never block or shorten them.
7.3 The sensor's field of view is unobstructed by any part of the housing.A sensor that is blocked gives wrong data, so the design fails its main job.
7.4 Sensors and servos are replaceable with hand tools in under 2 minutes.Classroom robots are reused across cohorts, so parts will wear and break.
Material8.1 PETG for the eye housing and yoke; 3 mm MDF for the mount plate.PETG is more impact-resistant than PLA and the lab printer is PETG-calibrated.
8.2 Flat or simple parts use 3 mm laser-cut MDF to save print time.MDF cutting is faster than printing for flat parts, freeing the printer for complex geometry.

B2 · Design Ideas 3 different ideas

Three ideas that differ in mechanism and form, not just looks. Each sketch is an isometric render of the 3D CAD model with the sensor and mount labelled.

Concept 1 — “Gimbal-Eye” SELECTED

Pan-tilt eyeball

A spherical eye in a yoke is moved by pan and tilt servos like a real eye, so the camera can look anywhere without the body moving.

Envelope 110.0 × 120.0 × 132.0 mm · ≈ 108 g · ≈ AED 38

Concept 2 — “Compound-Eye”

Insect dome of lens tubes

A dome of lens tubes copies an insect eye for a wide view. Eye-catching, but only one lens is a real camera so the rest are decoration.

Envelope 138.8 × 125.8 × 117.4 mm · ≈ 194 g · ≈ AED 43

Concept 3 — “Periscope-Cam”

Raised sliding mast camera

A camera on a motorised mast can rise above obstacles like a submarine periscope. Great view height, but a tall mast can tip the robot.

Envelope 100.0 × 80.0 × 193.0 mm · ≈ 74 g · ≈ AED 27

Modelling Worksheet — Making the Physical Model

Follow in order. Lab tools: 3D printer (PETG), laser cutter, M3 hex driver, 320-grit sandpaper, hot glue gun.

  1. Check the CAD: open the chosen-design STL in TinkerCAD or your slicer, set units to millimetres and confirm the envelope is 110.0 × 120.0 × 132.0 mm (limit 120 × 130 × 150 mm).
  2. Slice the printed parts (Brow, Eyeball, Iris ring, Yoke arm, Yoke bridge) in Cura/PrusaSlicer: PETG, 0.2 mm layers, 15–20% infill, supports only where an overhang needs them.
  3. Laser-cut the MDF parts (mount plate) from 3 mm MDF using the footprint on the top orthographic view; add 4 × M3 clearance holes for the UGOT mount.
  4. De-burr and sand every printed part (320 grit); pay attention to pivot holes and sensor windows so nothing binds or blocks a lens.
  5. Fit the sensor: Seat the camera module in the eye socket with the lens centred in the front opening, then add a clear window or a 2 mm recess to protect the glass.
  6. Assemble the mechanism following the numbered exploded view and BOM: fasteners finger-tight first, then check every moving part swings freely.
  7. Wire and test: connect the kit sensor and servo to the UGOT controller; run a short program to confirm each reading and movement.
  8. Mount to UGOT: bolt the mount plate to the chassis with the kit's M3 screws (Customer spec 3.1) and confirm the wheels still clear.
  9. Function-test against specs 7.1 and 7.2: Pan covers 180° and tilt covers 60° in under 3 seconds. Record results in your portfolio.
  10. Finish: spray or vinyl-wrap in the colour scheme from specification 1.1, then re-test fit after finishing.
